AI, Fat Tails, and the Economics of R&D

Most enterprises think about R&D in terms of projects: How much should we invest? Which projects should we fund? What return should we expect?

There is another useful way to think about it: R&D is a sampling problem.

Imagine that the returns from R&D projects follow a probability distribution. If those returns behaved roughly like a Gaussian distribution, extreme outcomes would be extraordinarily rare. Most projects would cluster around an average outcome, and very large successes would occur with vanishingly small probability.

But innovation does not necessarily behave this way.

A large number of experiments may produce little value. Some will fail completely. A small number may work reasonably well. And occasionally, one discovery, product, algorithm, material, or process improvement can generate value disproportionate to everything else in the portfolio.

This is much closer to the intuition of a fat-tailed distribution.

R&D as Tail Sampling

Suppose an enterprise runs 100 R&D experiments.

Perhaps 70 create negligible economic value. Twenty produce modest improvements. Nine generate meaningful returns. And one creates a breakthrough worth more than the other 99 experiments combined.

In such a system, asking only about the “average project” misses much of the economics.

The objective becomes partly about creating enough intelligent experiments to encounter the tail.

This leads to an important distinction between conventional operational optimization and R&D. In many operational processes, reducing variance is desirable. We want manufacturing quality, delivery time, or equipment reliability to become predictable.

In research, however, some variance is precisely what we are looking for. We deliberately explore uncertain possibilities because occasionally the outcome lies far away from what we already know.

The challenge is that we do not know in advance which experiment will produce that outcome.

AI Changes the Sampling Rate

This is where AI becomes economically interesting.

Suppose an organization can meaningfully evaluate only 20 ideas per year. Even if its R&D return distribution contains extraordinary outcomes, it may take years before the organization encounters one.

Now suppose AI allows researchers to formulate hypotheses, search literature, generate designs, write and test code, analyze experimental results, simulate alternatives, and eliminate weak candidates much faster.

The underlying uncertainty has not disappeared.

What has changed is the sampling rate.

Instead of conducting 20 meaningful iterations per year, perhaps the organization can conduct 100.

If valuable outcomes live in the tail of the distribution, increasing the number of well-designed experiments increases the organization's opportunities to encounter those outcomes.

This suggests a different way of thinking about AI productivity.

The value of AI in R&D may not primarily come from doing the same research with fewer people. It may come from compressing the experiment-learn-iterate cycle.

Faster Sampling Creates Faster Learning

There is another consequence.

Each experiment generates information.

An unsuccessful experiment is not necessarily wasted if it tells us something about the problem. It eliminates hypotheses, changes our understanding of the search space, and influences the next experiment.

So the process is not simply:

Experiment → Outcome

It is:

Experiment → Observation → Learning → Better Experiment → Observation → Learning

AI can accelerate this entire feedback loop.

That means the benefit of increasing experimentation may be greater than simply multiplying the number of independent attempts. The organization is not drawing blindly from the same distribution every time. Ideally, it is learning how to search the distribution better.

The effective process therefore combines two mechanisms:

more samples per unit time + better-directed samples over time.

The Strategic Implication

This changes how enterprises should evaluate AI investments in R&D.

If management evaluates every AI initiative using the expected return of an individual project, it may systematically undervalue experimentation.

For a heavy-tailed innovation process, the economically relevant object is often the portfolio of experiments and the speed at which the portfolio learns.

The question therefore shifts from:

“What is the ROI of this AI experiment?”

to:

“How much does AI increase the rate at which we can run, learn from, and redirect meaningful experiments?”

For operational AI, efficiency may be the primary objective.

For R&D AI, sampling velocity may be the more important metric.

When returns are fat-tailed, the organization that learns to sample intelligently and rapidly gets more opportunities to discover the rare outcomes that matter disproportionately.

In that sense, AI does more than automate R&D.

It changes the economics of searching the tail.
